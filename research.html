Research Overview

When is a planned route no longer worth following?

A path can be collision-free on paper and still become a poor decision in execution. My research explores how autonomous robots can recognize this mismatch and revise plans without losing the benefits of efficient low-level control.

Explore projects ↗Discuss the work

The problem

Navigation systems often optimize for a route that is feasible with the available map. But a robot sees only part of its surroundings, has limited compute, and may get stuck even if the policy continues producing actions confidently. The research question is not only how to find a path, but how to tell when the path or its assumptions should be revised.

What I have worked on

Decoupled RL–LLM navigation

Separating fast reinforcement-learning control from occasional language-model planning, with a displacement-based trigger for stalled progress and a structured grid interface to the model. The published evaluation reports 95% mean success over five map levels for the combined approach, compared with 72% for pure PPO among the learning/reactive baselines.

Kinematic-aware route optimization

In the KA-IHO study, path optimization accounts for motion feasibility rather than only grid-level collision avoidance. The work combines optimization refinements with trajectory smoothing and hardware evaluation.

Current research direction

Plan repair versus replanning

How can a robot distinguish a short-lived execution disturbance from evidence that its planned route is fundamentally outdated? Useful signals may include progress toward the goal, repeated constraint activation, and the extent of local deviation needed to retain feasibility.

An initial investigation can compare event-triggered replanning against fixed-interval replanning and simple threshold baselines on moving-obstacle and changing-corridor scenarios. Outcomes should include completion, collisions, redundant replanning and compute cost. This is a research direction, not an established result.
